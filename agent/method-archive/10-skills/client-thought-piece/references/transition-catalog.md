# Transition Catalog: Connective Tissue for Financial Argument

Researched 2026-08-19 from the craft canon (Williams' *Style*, McPhee's *Draft No. 4*, Zinsser, Pinker's *Sense of Style*, Strunk & White, Orwell) and the working repertoire of Levine, Lewis, Buffett, Housel, Kay, and The Economist's leaders. Applied structurally: choose a move per joint because of what the joint needs, never pepper.

## Canon, compressed

- **Williams (given/new):** cohesion is starting sentences with what the reader already has (usually the previous paragraph's ending) and ending on the new. Most transitions should be invisible information flow. Needing "Moreover" means the sentence order is wrong.
- **McPhee:** transitions are a symptom of structure. Heavy mortar means the parts are in the wrong order; reorder instead of caulking. White space is a legitimate transition.
- **Zinsser:** mood changers (But, Yet, Still, Instead) first in the sentence; the last sentence of each paragraph is the springboard to the next.
- **Pinker:** a connective is needed only when the coherence relation is guessable wrong. Perform structure, don't announce it.

## The move catalog (pick one per joint, vary across a piece and a set)

1. **Backward-glance pickup.** Open on a word or phrase the previous paragraph ended with. (Founding example: "...for anyone who wants to trace it." → "Anyone who wants to trace it. That phrase would have meant something to an earlier generation of market people.")
2. **Mood-changer pivot.** But / Yet / Still / Instead, sentence-initial. Never "However,".
3. **Concession-turn.** Grant the objection, keep the point. "Of course, a protocol that only ever said no would be a museum."
4. **Fair-objection set piece.** Stage the skeptic's best case, concede it honestly, answer it. "A fair objection here is that shame was load-bearing... True. But shame only worked until the partners could sell."
5. **Economist leader turn.** A paragraph of genuine praise, then a hard "Yet" paragraph. The praise must be real pre-payment.
6. **Zoom.** Wide claim → one person, one number, one Tuesday.
7. **Zoom-out.** After the anecdote, one sentence naming what it was an instance of.
8. **Return-from-digression.** A planned tangent with a visible return ("Anyway. The vaults."). Requires a strong spine to return to.
9. **Rhetorical handoff.** Name the next problem as a consequence of the last ("Which brings us to the problem nobody writes into the term sheet").
10. **Reader-address.** "Notice what's missing from that list." Directs attention; doesn't decorate.
11. **Time-jump.** "For a while, that worked." / "That was March."
12. **Hedge-then-commit.** Concede imprecision, assert direction. ("Roughly speaking... The direction is not in doubt.")
13. **Deflation move.** Follow the grand claim with a dry practical sentence. ("Follow enough of these decisions and a personality emerges... and it is not an exciting one.")
14. **Question hinge.** End a paragraph on the question the next one answers. Ration: this becomes the catechism tell at density.
15. **Escalation ladder.** "That was the small problem." / "All of that is operational discipline, and operational discipline decays unless something harder stands behind it."
16. **Aphorism hinge.** A standalone one-liner between blocks that looks backward and forward at once. Ration hard (see tell registry).
17. **Recurring-object thread (Lewis).** Plant a concrete object or phrase early; its reappearance IS the transition. ("An ordinary day" planted in the vetting story, returning as the final beat.)
18. **Confession pivot (Buffett).** Turn sections on an admitted error or bias. Buys credibility exactly where the argument turns.
19. **Dry enumeration reset.** When the argument tangles, restart schematic: "The basic story is: (1)... (2)... (3)..." The flatness is itself a tonal transition.
20. **Negative-space cut.** Section break, then open cold in a new scene or register. The reader supplies the connective and feels smart doing it.

## Placement principles

1. Mortar only where the relation is guessable wrong; obvious relations need nothing.
2. The cheapest mortar is invisible: rewrite the new paragraph's first sentence to start from the old paragraph's ending (fixes most bare joints with zero added words).
3. Edit joints in pairs: previous paragraph's last sentence + next paragraph's first sentence, together.
4. A joint needing a full sentence of throat-clearing means the paragraphs are in the wrong order. Reorder.
5. Cut hard at changes of scene, time, or register; mortar within continuous argument.
6. One-line hinge paragraphs: at most ~2 per 1,000 words.
7. Vary joint types across the piece and give each piece in a set ONE set-piece turn, different per piece.
8. Pre-pay hard cuts: plant the object or line earlier that makes the later cut land clean. The fix for a weak joint is often two pages upstream.

## Anti-patterns (never)

Additive stacks (Additionally/Moreover/Furthermore); "It's worth noting"; announced structure; symmetric pivots ("It's not about X. It's about Y."); concession-shaped noise ("That said," with nothing conceded); every paragraph opening on a traffic-signal adverb; uniform joint density. The litmus test: delete the connective phrase; if nothing changes about what the reader believes connects the paragraphs, it was filler.

## What the pros do that guides miss

- **Levine:** transitions are tonal before they are logical; a temperature change (deadpan after complexity) marks a new unit better than any adverb. Digressions are planned, with the return as an intimacy move.
- **Lewis:** joints ride on objects and people, not propositions; recognition replaces logic. Dramatic-irony joints ("What he didn't yet know...") convert topic changes into suspense.
- **Buffett:** connective tissue is relational; re-establishing who is talking to whom (direct address, confession, folksy analogy) works as a warm section break.
- **All three:** they trust white space because they pre-paid for it earlier with planted objects, schematics, or aphorisms.
