# Email archetype taxonomy and sequence architecture (reference method)

Reference method for copy work: seven email archetypes, blend mode, the source's structural craft layer, and sequence architecture. Use alongside the five-pass routine and the review pipeline; those and his voice rules govern the output.

This draft retains the source method's substance, sequence, and detail per the owner's preserve directive (2026-11-11: "keep as much from the originals as it's useful"). Adaptations from the source are listed at the end and are limited to removing IB-platform behavior and IB's founder voice, per the do-not-activate list.

## Core idea

Every email is one of seven archetypes. Each archetype is a distinct primary energy with its own purpose, structure, voice, subject-line style, and guardrails. The power is in the range, not in any single email: most senders have one gear ("hey, buy this"); seven archetypes give seven distinct energies. Archetypes can be blended (one primary, one secondary). A sequence is a deliberate progression of archetypes — an emotional arc from connection to close — not a blast.

Beliefs the method states:

- The email IS the relationship. Every email strengthens or weakens it; there is no neutral.
- Message match is sacred. The landing page makes a promise; the first email fulfills it exactly. Break the chain and the reader is lost.
- Voice has two layers: a structural voice (how emails work — rhythm, pacing, patterns; always on) and a personality voice (who is speaking — the sender's own). The archetype sets the shape; the personality fills it.
- Sequences are journeys, not blasts. Each email earns the right to send the next.

## The seven archetypes

### 1. The Love Letter

- Energy: warm, personal, generous. "I made this for you." Reads like a handwritten note from someone who cares.
- Purpose: build deep emotional bond; make the reader feel seen; create open-every-email loyalty.
- When to use: welcome emails; after a big sales push (return to human mode); when giving something away; when the list feels like a number.
- Structure: (1) personal opening — timestamp, setting, observation; (2) the heart — what you actually feel or give; (3) the connection — how this relates to them; (4) warm close — soft CTA or gift, or just a genuine sign-off.
- Voice shift: slower rhythm, slightly longer sentences, more "I", less bold, fewer bullets. Journal-entry feel.
- Subject style: lowercase, personal, no hype ("something I've been thinking about", "a quick note", "this one's for you").
- Guardrails: CTA natural, not bolted on; under ~300 words pure; no forced product mention; no urgency language in a pure Love Letter.

### 2. The Value Drop

- Energy: generous, authoritative, immediately useful. Pure teaching — the reader can DO something within 10 minutes.
- Purpose: build authority through generosity; create reciprocity; give a quick win.
- When to use: a genuine insight or tactic; between pitches (earning the right to sell later); positioning as the obvious expert; warming a cold audience.
- Structure: (1) hook — the insight stated directly; (2) the framework/tactic — specific, actionable; (3) quick implementation — "how to use this in the next 10 minutes"; (4) soft close — no pitch, may hint at depth.
- Voice shift: teacher energy. Numbered steps, arrows, bold keywords, punchy and scannable, more "you" than "I".
- Subject style: benefit-forward, specific ("3 things that actually work for [goal]", "How to [outcome] in [timeframe]").
- Guardrails: value within the first 3 sentences; specific, not vague tips; implementable in under 5 steps; no product pitch — the value IS the selling.

### 3. The Story Bridge

- Energy: narrative flow. The reader is absorbed in a story and only realizes it connects to an offer when already nodding. (Source names Chaperon's Sideways Sales Letter and Brunson's Epiphany Bridge as its bases.)
- Purpose: sell contextually — connect a real story to an offer through narrative.
- When to use: a strong story that shows why the product matters; warm-not-hot readers; selling without "pitching"; mid-sequence bridges from value to offer.
- Structure: (1) story hook — a specific scene, sensory detail; (2) the narrative — tension, struggle; (3) the turn — insight or moment of change; (4) the bridge — natural connection to the offer; (5) one CTA, woven into the story's conclusion.
- Voice shift: most narrative archetype; longer paragraphs when building a scene; less bold, fewer lists.
- Subject style: curiosity, story teaser ("The $2,400 lesson that changed everything").
- Guardrails: story BEFORE product; bridge organic; the story worth reading without the CTA; don't rush to the sell; one CTA, one link, one action.

### 4. The Conversation Starter

- Energy: short, punchy, disarming. Feels like a text from a friend. Aims to get replies.
- Purpose: re-engage a quiet list; warm the audience before a launch; build dialogue. The source claims replies also improve deliverability (replies signaling engagement to ESPs) — that deliverability benefit is the source's claim, unverified by us.
- Structure: (1) one short opening line; (2) 1-2 sentences of context (optional); (3) the ask — a direct "Reply and tell me X"; (4) sign-off. Total 3-7 sentences.
- Voice shift: most casual; fragments; no headers, bold, or formatting; typed-quickly feel.
- Subject style: ultra-short, personal ("Quick question", "What are you working on?", ".").
- Guardrails: under 75 words; no links, images, or formatting; one request, one reply; no setup paragraphs.

### 5. The Proof

- Energy: credibility, aspiration. "That could be me." Social proof through someone else's transformation, told as narrative.
- Purpose: create desire via a real customer story — their lived experience, not your claims.
- When to use: a customer story with specific results; before or during a launch; overcoming skepticism; when your claims would sound like bragging.
- Structure: (1) set the scene — who is this person, relatable; (2) the problem; (3) the discovery; (4) the result — specific numbers and timeframes; (5) the reflection — their actual words; (6) the mirror — the reader sees themselves; (7) soft CTA.
- Voice shift: third-person, journalistic; facts, quotes, specifics; the subject is the hero.
- Subject style: name + result ("How Bryan went from browsing to building in 7 days").
- Guardrails: real names, numbers, timeframes; product is the tool, not the star; not a feature list; include their actual words.

### 6. The Squeeze

- Energy: direct, urgent, honest closing. Respectful-direct, not sleazy. You've earned the right to ask.
- Purpose: close the sale around a real deadline; end of a value sequence.
- Structure (integrates PAS, value equation, honest urgency): (1) direct opening — state the situation; (2) offer restated in one sentence; (3) the math — value vs. cost, specific numbers; (4) the objection killer — handle the one remaining "but..."; (5) the deadline — specific, real, honest; (6) one clear CTA; (7) P.S. — deadline reminder or final proof point.
- Voice shift: most direct; shortest sentences; most bold; confident, not desperate.
- Subject style: urgency, direct ("[X] hours left", "Before the price goes up").
- Guardrails: urgency MUST be real — fake deadlines destroy trust permanently; one CTA destination; exactly one objection handled; never apologetic about selling; keep the math simple.

### 7. The Belief Breaker

- Energy: contrarian, intellectual. Makes the reader reconsider something taken for granted.
- Purpose: shift beliefs so the eventual offer feels inevitable; dislodge a belief blocking the sale; differentiate from conventional wisdom.
- Structure: (1) the common belief, stated fairly — no strawmen; (2) the crack — the one thing that doesn't make sense; (3) the reframe — your alternative, with evidence; (4) the implication for them specifically; (5) optional seed of what to do about it.
- Voice shift: most intellectual; philosophical framing and historical parallels; longer sentences when building an argument.
- Subject style: contrarian hook ("You don't need a great idea", "Stop [common advice]. Do this instead.").
- Guardrails: substantive, not clickbait; present the common belief fairly first; use evidence; no product pitch in a pure Belief Breaker; not preachy.

## Blend mode

Real emails often blend two energies:

- Primary archetype sets structure and dominant energy (60-70%); secondary adds flavor (30-40%). The primary's structure template is used; the secondary's voice and techniques layer in.
- Effective blends from the source: Love Letter + Squeeze (warm email, genuine urgency — price increases for loyal subscribers); Value Drop + Story Bridge (teach through narrative toward an offer — mid-sequence); Proof + Squeeze (customer story with urgency — final launch emails); Belief Breaker + Story Bridge (contrarian reframe told as a story that sells — opening emails); Love Letter + Value Drop (welcome emails with substance); Value Drop + Conversation Starter (engagement booster).
- Blend rules: the primary energy immediately recognizable; the secondary enhances, never competes; if confused, default to pure primary; the result must read as ONE email, not two stapled together.

## Structural voice (the source's "always on" craft layer)

These are the source method's genre rules for how it says great emails work — IB's method rules, presented here as proposals pending ruling, not Alejandro's current style and not adopted house rules. Nothing in this draft is active anywhere.

Rhythm: short paragraphs (1-3 sentences, often 1); fragment sentences as their own paragraphs for impact; bold for key phrases, not decoration; dashes for bullets; arrows for steps; white space everywhere; one-word paragraphs when something must land.

Structural patterns:

1. The graceful out — every selling email gives permission to say no. Makes selling honest.
2. Comparison math in Squeeze emails — show the numbers; make value obvious.
3. Dual-path structure — two contrasting realities; the reader chooses (Squeeze, Story Bridge, Belief Breaker).
4. Honest disclaimers inside selling — acknowledge limits; never hide the truth.
5. Scene-painting in future projection — the exact after-state: specific time, place, feeling (Story Bridge, Love Letter, Proof).
6. P.S. substance rule — the P.S. earns its place: a resource, link, reframe, or reply request. Never filler.
7. One CTA per email — one purpose, one action. Exception: repeating the SAME link.

Formatting: subject lines under 50 characters; no ALL CAPS; preview text (first 40-60 characters) counts; subject style matches the archetype; for blends, the primary archetype's style.

## Sequence architecture

The archetype progression IS the strategy.

Principles: never repeat an archetype back-to-back; the sequence has a shape (connection → value + belief → urgency + action); each email earns the next; you don't sell in email 1 — you earn the right to sell by email 5.

Source sequence shapes:

Welcome / lead-magnet delivery (5-7 emails):
1. Love Letter (deliver the magnet, welcome them)
2. Belief Breaker + Value Drop blend (reframe how they think)
3. Value Drop (pure teaching, quick win)
4. Story Bridge (customer success → bridge to offer)
5. Conversation Starter (replies, relationship)
6. Proof + subtle offer mention
7. Love Letter + Squeeze blend (warm close with deadline)

Launch / sales (4-6 emails):
1. Belief Breaker (reframe the problem)
2. Story Bridge (transformation story → offer)
3. Value Drop (prove competence)
4. Proof (specific numbers)
5. Squeeze (deadline, math, one CTA)
6. Love Letter + Squeeze blend (final personal appeal)

Nurture (ongoing): rotate Value Drop → Story Bridge → Belief Breaker → Love Letter → Conversation Starter; occasional Proof when results exist; Squeeze ONLY at a real deadline.

The Email 1 rule (message match): if the sequence follows a landing-page opt-in, Email 1 must (1) reference the CTA promise from the page, (2) deliver what was promised, (3) set expectations for what comes next, (4) sound like the same person who wrote the page.
